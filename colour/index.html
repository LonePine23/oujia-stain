A Game of Hueristics

Mix a colour from nothing but its name. The closer your mix, the higher you score.

Challenge
By Geordie Crawley
Reference Huephoria

Three faders, one name, no clues.

How to play

Each round hands you the name of a real colour and nothing else. No swatch, no hex, no multiple choice. Your job is to mix it from memory and instinct using three faders — hue (which colour, 0–360° around the wheel), saturation (how vivid, 0–100%) and lightness (how pale or dark, 0–100%).

The mat shows your mix live as you drag. When you're happy, lock it in — your swatch slides aside and the real colour appears next to it, on neutral grey so neither one skews how you read the other.

The hue fader carries a fixed spectrum because 0–360 is an arbitrary scale that needs a legend. Saturation and lightness don't get one: everybody already knows which end is grey and which end is white.

Colour names are taken from an open-source database of nearly five thousand of them, so expect anything from Oxblood to Hello Darkness My Old Friend.

Modes

  • Challenge — 5, 10 or 15 rounds, drawn at random from the whole list.
  • Daily — five colours chosen by the date, so every player gets the same five on the same day. Your first run each day is recorded; play again as much as you like, but only that first one counts toward your stats and streak.
  • Completionist — every colour in the list, one at a time, in a fixed order. Progress is saved on this device, so you can stop whenever and pick it up later. The bar along the top fills in left to right with the actual colours you've cleared.

Scoring

Scoring happens in CIE Lab, not in the numbers on the faders. Raw slider distance would be badly misleading: hue wraps around, so 359° and 1° are neighbours rather than opposites, and a 30° hue miss is glaring on a vivid red but almost invisible on a near-grey. Lab handles both, because it's built to track how different two colours actually look.

The distance between your mix and the target is ΔE — 0 is a perfect match, and anything past about 80 is a different colour entirely. That gets curved into a match percentage:

96Indistinguishable
87Within a whisker
76A very good match
60Close
44Off by a shade
28Wrong neighbourhood
16Not this time
0A different colour entirely

A session is scored on the average of its rounds, not the total, so a long game isn't automatically a better one.

Your colour

At the end of a session your rounds are boiled down into a single colour. The hue comes from the average of everything you actually mixed, weighted so the vivid guesses count for more than the muddy ones — so it belongs to your run and nobody else's. How well you scored drives the rest: play sharply and it arrives clear and bright, play badly and it comes out murky. Then the name list tells you what you are.
