A Game of Hueristics

You're given a colour's name. Mix it from memory, one guess at a time, until you land on the real thing. Fewer guesses is better.

Free play
By Geordie Crawley
Reference Huephoria

One colour. As few guesses as you can manage.

How to play

You're given the name of a real colour and nothing else — no swatch, no hex. Mix what you think it looks like using three faders: hue (which colour, 0–360° around the wheel), saturation (how vivid, 0–100%) and lightness (how pale or dark, 0–100%). Then lock the guess in.

Every guess tells you two things: how close you got, and the name of the colour you actually mixed. That second one is the real clue. If you're chasing “Oxblood” and the game tells you you've just mixed “Manatee”, you've learned you're sitting in the greys and need to go redder and darker.

Guesses are unlimited and they stack up in a list below the faders, so you can see the whole trail. The faders stay where you left them — you're refining, not starting over.

Colour names are taken from an open-source database of nearly five thousand of them, so expect anything from Oxblood to Hello Darkness My Old Friend.

Modes

  • Daily — one colour, the same one for everyone, changing at midnight. Your first attempt each day is the one that counts; play again afterwards as much as you like and it won't be recorded.
  • Practice — a random colour, as often as you want.
  • Completionist — every colour in the list, one at a time, in a fixed order. Progress is saved on this device, so you can stop whenever and pick it up later.

Scoring

Closeness is measured in CIE Lab, not in the numbers on the faders. Raw slider distance would be badly misleading: hue wraps around, so 359° and 1° are neighbours rather than opposites, and a 30° hue miss is glaring on a vivid red but almost invisible on a near-grey. Lab is built to track how different two colours actually look.

The gap between your mix and the target is ΔE. Zero is a perfect match; anything past about 80 is a different colour entirely. It's also shown as a percentage, which is easier to read at a glance:

96Indistinguishable
87Within a whisker
76A very good match
60Close
44Off by a shade
28Wrong neighbourhood
16Not this time
0A different colour entirely

You've solved it when the gap falls to ΔE 2 or less — the point at which two colours side by side are, to an ordinary pair of eyes, the same colour. Chasing an exact match on all three faders would mean hunting single degrees for no visible gain.

The only number that matters at the end is how many guesses it took. There's no par and no bonus for elegance.

If you get stuck

After ten guesses you can ask for one hint, which names the fader you're furthest off on — but not which way to move it. After fifteen you can reveal the answer outright. Revealing ends the puzzle unsolved: it won't join your guess distribution, and it breaks a daily streak.

Your colour

At the end you get the whole route: every guess in order, closing in on the target. Underneath, the wrong turns are averaged into a colour of their own — hue from everything you mixed on the way, weighted so the vivid detours count for more than the muddy ones — and the name list says what it was. The more scenic your journey, the more interesting the souvenir.
