How it works

Find the colour in the photo.

The three modes

Difficulty is about what you're given, not how you're marked.

  • Easy — the colour chip, its name, and a loupe split down the middle: the target on one side, the pixel under your marker on the other, meeting at a straight edge. When the join disappears you're on it. This is the most sensitive comparison there is, which is why it's the easy one.
  • Medium — the chip, its name, and a magnifier ringed in the target colour. You judge your pixel against the photograph itself rather than against a flat field, and you can change the magnification.
  • Hard — the name and nothing else. No chip, no hex code, and the loupe barrel is plain grey so it gives nothing away. You have to already know what Glaucous looks like. The chip appears once you've locked in, so you find out either way.

Playing a round

You're given a colour — a swatch, its name and its hex. Somewhere in the photograph is a point that matches it.

  • Tap or click anywhere to drop a marker.
  • Drag to move it. On a keyboard, arrow keys move one pixel and Shift-arrow moves ten.
  • The loupe follows the marker, in whichever form your mode allows. On Medium you can switch between the two.
  • Lock in when you're satisfied. There's no clock.

How it's scored

Your colour is compared to the target with CIEDE2000, the international standard for colour difference. It's measured in ΔE, which is roughly the number of just-noticeable differences between two colours. It isn't a distance in RGB — it's tuned to how an eye actually reads a gap, so two dark browns can be far apart and two bright yellows can be almost the same.

Every mode is marked on the same curve, so a score means the same thing whoever you're playing against.

Where you clicked doesn't matter — only the colour you landed on. If the target colour appears in three places in the photograph, all three are equally right. The marked target point is just one place it's known to be.

Colours are averaged over a small disc rather than read from a single pixel, so one stray speck of JPEG noise can't decide a round.

When you lock in, the photograph drains to grey except for the colour you were hunting, which stays at full strength everywhere it occurs — every place that would have scored 80 or better. It answers the question the marker can't: not where it was, but where it all was.

The colours themselves

Nothing is pre-set. Each round the photograph is analysed and a colour is drawn from it at random, weighted towards regions that are vivid and big enough to find without being the entire background. The same photograph will hand you something different next time.

Your colour

At the end, the session resolves into a single colour and you're told what it's called. Its hue comes from the average of everything you actually picked, so it belongs to that run. Its lightness and saturation come from your accuracy: play well and it arrives clear and vivid, play badly and it comes out murky.
